GaussianRenderFormer: Transformer-based Rendering of 3D Gaussian Splats



Shahaf Valencia Levy  shahaf.valenciale@mail.huji.ac.il

Affiliation: The Hebrew University of Jerusalem

  
Sagie Benaim  sagie.benaim@mail.huji.ac.il

Abstract
    

Can a neural network learn to render 3D Gaussian splats on its own, with no rasterizer in the renderer? We present GaussianRenderFormer, a transformer that takes a set of Gaussians and a camera and outputs the image directly. Each Gaussian becomes one token in a view-independent encoder, and a view-dependent decoder turns small patches of camera rays into pixels by attending to those tokens. Trained on 26,820 Objaverse objects pruned to 20,000 Gaussians each, GaussianRenderFormer renders 300 unseen objects at 38.08 dB PSNR against the full, unpruned splats, while rasterizing the same pruned input reaches 44.02 dB. The gap is not one of generalization, and our diagnostics are consistent with a bottleneck in the view decoder: telling it the projected footprint of each Gaussian, which it could in principle infer, adds 1.4 dB. Under-training is not ruled out. Code, trained models and videos: https://svlwoof.github.io/gaussianrenderformer.

1 Introduction




3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) (Kerbl et al., 2023) has become a common way to represent real scenes captured from
photos. A 3DGS scene is a set of Gaussians, each with a position, scale, rotation, color and opacity, and every image
of it is produced by a rasterizer: a hand-written algorithm that projects the Gaussians onto the image plane and blends
them. In this paper we replace that algorithm with a neural network.11
            1
            
            
            
          Large language models assisted
both the research behind this paper, including code and the analysis of experiments, and its writing. The authors
take full responsibility for its content. Given a set of Gaussians and a camera, our model
outputs the image directly, without per-scene optimization and with no rasterizer in the renderer
(Figure 1).




Learned rendering has made significant progress for other kinds of scene descriptions.
RenderFormer (Zeng et al., 2025) renders triangle meshes with global illumination using a transformer, and
RenderFormer-V2 (Zeng et al., 2026) extends it to more kinds of primitives, lights and media. For Gaussians,
to our knowledge, no learned renderer exists. Methods that reconstruct, refine, relight or analyze Gaussians still hand the final image to a hand-written renderer, usually the 3DGS rasterizer itself
(Section 2).




A learned renderer of Gaussians could offer what a rasterizer does not. It could learn light transport from data, as
RenderFormer does for meshes, where relightable Gaussian methods rely on a hand-written shading model and approximate
the indirect light (Gao et al., 2024; Liang et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025a). It could render captured splats together
with meshes and lights in one model, as RenderFormer-V2 does for meshes, media and lights. It has no sorting step, so it
avoids the popping that sorting causes as the camera moves. And it could learn a prior that fills in what its input lacks. None of this is
shown here: a network first has to render Gaussians at all.




It is also not obvious that the approach that works for meshes carries over to Gaussians. Gaussians have no surface
or material, are semi-transparent and overlap heavily. An object typically needs tens of
thousands, while RenderFormer was trained on scenes of up to 4,096 triangles. A learned renderer has to
resolve which Gaussians are visible and how they blend at every pixel, which a rasterizer does by sorting
them.




We present GaussianRenderFormer, a transformer that renders Gaussians directly. Each Gaussian becomes one token, and a
view-independent encoder lets these tokens exchange information. A view-dependent decoder then represents each small
patch of the image by a token built from the camera rays passing through it. These tokens attend to the Gaussians and
are told where in the image each Gaussian lands and how large it appears there, both computed per Gaussian, while
everything per pixel is learned. We start from RenderFormer’s pretrained weights and train on 26,820
objects from Objaverse (Deitke et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2025), each pruned to 20,000 Gaussians.




We treat this as a feasibility study at the object level. On 300 objects never seen in training, GaussianRenderFormer reaches
38.08 dB PSNR against renders of the full, unpruned splats, while rasterizing the same pruned input reaches 44.02 dB
(Section 6). For context, LVSM (Jin et al., 2025), the closest rasterizer-free transformer,
reaches 32.36 dB with its decoder-only variant on Google Scanned Objects at 512 pixels from four input images, and GS-LRM (Zhang et al., 2024),
which rasterizes, 30.52 dB, both as reported in LVSM’s Table 1. They start from images and we from the splat, so the numbers are not directly comparable,
but they show the range in which learned renderers of objects work. The gap to rasterization is not one of
generalization, and our diagnostics are consistent with a bottleneck in the view decoder, though under-training is
not ruled out.




Our contributions are (1) GaussianRenderFormer, to our knowledge the first feed-forward neural renderer for 3D Gaussian splats
with no rasterizer in the renderer, (2) three changes that adapt a mesh renderer’s view decoder to Gaussians, a 2D
rotary embedding of where each Gaussian lands in the image, its projected footprint on the attention keys and smaller
patches, each ablated at moderate scale and again at full scale, together with a schedule that trains the new input
layer first, and (3) an analysis of the remaining gap to rasterization, which is not a generalization gap and is
consistent with a bottleneck in the view decoder, and of how the model behaves outside its training setup. The code and the trained models are available at https://github.com/SVLwoof/gaussianformer.





2 Related Work




Rendering 3D Gaussians. 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) renders a scene by projecting each Gaussian onto the image plane and alpha-blending the
sorted projections, a process evolved from EWA splatting (Zwicker et al., 2002). Later works improve this rasterizer
against aliasing (Yu et al., 2024), with flat disks (Huang et al., 2024) or with per-pixel
sorting (Radl et al., 2024), and gsplat (Ye et al., 2025)22
            2
            
            
            
          https://github.com/nerfstudio-project/gsplat
is the optimized open-source implementation we use to render our ground truth. Other works ray-trace Gaussians (Moenne-Loccoz et al., 2024)
or replace them with ellipsoids that can be ray-traced exactly (Mai et al., 2025), and Sort-free Gaussian Splatting (Hou et al., 2025) learns
weights that replace the sorting but keeps the projection and blending of a rasterizer. All of these are hand-written
algorithms, while GaussianRenderFormer renders with a network.




Learned renderers for explicit scenes. The works closest to ours render an explicit description of a scene with a network instead of an algorithm.
RenderFormer renders triangle meshes with global illumination (Section 3.2), and
RenderFormer-V2 (Zeng et al., 2026) and RenderFormer++ (Du et al., 2026) extend it to more primitives, lights and media and to
object-level tokens, but none of them takes Gaussians. Pointersect (Chang et al., 2023) renders point clouds
with a transformer per ray, Xu et al. (2026) predict indirect illumination from sampled points, and
RenderNet (Nguyen-Phuoc et al., 2018) rendered voxel grids and the Generative Query
Network (Eslami et al., 2018) a learned scene code. Most other neural rendering methods keep a conventional renderer and
are optimized per scene: deferred neural rendering (Thies et al., 2019) and neural point-based
graphics (Aliev et al., 2020; Rückert et al., 2022; Franke et al., 2024) rasterize learned features before a network produces
the image, and Point-NeRF (Xu et al., 2022) ray-marches neural points. To our knowledge, GaussianRenderFormer
is the first model to render a given set of 3D Gaussians with a network alone.




Networks around Gaussian splatting. Most learned models that involve 3D Gaussians produce them rather than render them. Feed-forward reconstruction
methods predict Gaussians from a set of images in a single pass and then rasterize
them (Szymanowicz et al., 2024; Charatan et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024; Tang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025c; Jiang et al., 2025b). Other models take Gaussians as input tokens for other tasks,
such as self-supervised pretraining (Ma et al., 2025), scene understanding (Li et al., 2025),
generative modeling (Gao et al., 2025) or refining the Gaussians themselves (Chen et al., 2025b), and
relightable methods shade them with a hand-written model (Gao et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2024; Liang et al., 2024), which GI-GS (Chen et al., 2025a) extends with an approximation of the indirect light. Wherever these
methods produce an image, a rasterizer still renders it, while for GaussianRenderFormer the Gaussians are the input and the image is the output.




View synthesis without explicit geometry. Another line of work synthesizes new views without any explicit 3D representation, mostly with transformers. The Scene
Representation Transformer (Sajjadi et al., 2022) decodes each pixel from a ray that attends to latent tokens of posed
images, Light Field Networks (Sitzmann et al., 2021), Light Field Neural Rendering (Suhail et al., 2022) and
GNT (Varma T et al., 2023) map rays to colors directly or with attention along each ray, and LVSM (Jin et al., 2025)
and RayZer (Jiang et al., 2025a) scale this idea to large models. These are the closest relatives of GaussianRenderFormer: most of them decode
scene tokens with ray queries, and none uses a rasterizer. Their scene, however, is given only through images, while ours
is explicit Gaussians, so our output can be compared directly with rasterizing the same input.





3 Background




3.1 3D Gaussian Splatting




3DGS represents a scene as a set of NN Gaussians, fitted to photos by gradient descent. Gaussian ii has a center
𝝁i∈ℝ3\bm{\mu}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}, a covariance 𝚺i=𝐑i​𝐒i​𝐒i⊤​𝐑i⊤\bm{\Sigma}_{i}=\mathbf{R}_{i}\mathbf{S}_{i}\mathbf{S}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{R}_{i}^{\top} built from a rotation 𝐑i\mathbf{R}_{i} (a unit quaternion 𝐪i\mathbf{q}_{i}) and
per-axis scales 𝐒i=diag⁡(𝐬i)\mathbf{S}_{i}=\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{s}_{i}), an opacity oi∈[0,1]o_{i}\in[0,1] and a color 𝐜i\mathbf{c}_{i}.
To render an image, the rasterizer projects each Gaussian to a 2D Gaussian with center 𝝁i′\bm{\mu}^{\prime}_{i} and
covariance 𝚺i′=𝐉𝐖​𝚺i​𝐖⊤​𝐉⊤\bm{\Sigma}^{\prime}_{i}=\mathbf{J}\mathbf{W}\bm{\Sigma}_{i}\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{J}^{\top}, where
𝐖\mathbf{W} is the camera’s rotation and 𝐉\mathbf{J} the Jacobian of the projection, sorts the Gaussians by depth
and blends them front to back at every pixel 𝐱\mathbf{x}:





𝐂⁡(𝐱)=∑i=1N𝐜i​αi​(𝐱)​∏j=1i−1(1−αj​(𝐱)),αi​(𝐱)=oi​exp⁡(−12​(𝐱−𝝁i′)⊤​𝚺i′−1​(𝐱−𝝁i′)).\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{c}_{i}\,\alpha_{i}(\mathbf{x})\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}\bigl(1-\alpha_{j}(\mathbf{x})\bigr),\qquad\alpha_{i}(\mathbf{x})=o_{i}\exp\Bigl(-\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x}-\bm{\mu}^{\prime}_{i})^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}^{\prime-1}_{i}(\mathbf{x}-\bm{\mu}^{\prime}_{i})\Bigr).

(1)


This is the computation GaussianRenderFormer replaces. Of the color, which 3DGS stores as spherical harmonics that change with
the viewing direction, we use only the view-independent part, a single RGB value, in the input and the ground truth.





3.2 RenderFormer




RenderFormer renders a triangle mesh with a transformer in two stages. In the view-independent stage, each triangle
becomes one token, built by a linear layer from its vertex normals and its material, and 12 layers of self-attention
over these tokens and 16 register tokens model how light travels between triangles. Positions enter only through a
rotary position embedding (Su et al., 2024) of the triangles’ vertices in the attention. In the view-dependent stage, each patch of
8×88\times 8 pixels becomes one token, built by a linear layer from the directions of its 64 rays. Six layers of
cross-attention to the triangle tokens, with the triangles’ positions in the camera’s
frame, each followed by self-attention among the ray-bundle tokens, gather what every patch sees, and a DPT
head (Ranftl et al., 2021) turns them into pixels of high dynamic range radiance, encoded as log10⁡(1+x)\log_{10}(1+x).
RenderFormer is trained on scenes of up to 4,096 triangles, first at 256 and then at 512 pixels.
GaussianRenderFormer keeps this layout and its pretrained weights (Section 4).

GaussianRenderFormer keeps RenderFormer’s two-stage layout (Section 3.2) and starts from its
pretrained weights. We change the first stage so that each token is a Gaussian instead of a
triangle, and how the second stage looks at the scene, so that it can handle tens of thousands of
semi-transparent Gaussians: it is told where each Gaussian lands in the image and how large it appears there.

4.1 Gaussian tokens and the scene encoder




Each Gaussian is described by a vector of 14 numbers,





𝐠i=[𝝁i,𝐬i,𝐪i,𝐜i,oi]∈ℝ14,\mathbf{g}_{i}=\bigl[\,\bm{\mu}_{i},\;\mathbf{s}_{i},\;\mathbf{q}_{i},\;\mathbf{c}_{i},\;o_{i}\,\bigr]\in\mathbb{R}^{14},

(2)


holding its center (3), scales (3), rotation quaternion (4), color (3) and opacity (1). The token of Gaussian ii is





𝐭i=𝐞+RMSNorm⁡(𝐀𝐠i+𝐛),\mathbf{t}_{i}=\mathbf{e}+\mathrm{RMSNorm}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{g}_{i}+\mathbf{b}),

(3)


where 𝐀∈ℝ768×14\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{768\times 14} and 𝐛\mathbf{b} are a linear layer followed by RMSNorm (Zhang & Sennrich, 2019), and 𝐞\mathbf{e} is a learned
embedding shared by all Gaussian tokens. The token embeds all 14 numbers, the center included, so it changes with the
random rotation of every training sample (Section 4.3). This input layer replaces RenderFormer’s
triangle embedding, and it is the only new part of the model apart from the footprint layers
(Section 4.2), which start at zero. In addition, the encoder’s
self-attention applies a 3D rotary position embedding of each Gaussian’s center, where RenderFormer used the
triangle’s vertices. The 16 register tokens sit at the mean
center of the Gaussians. The rest of the scene encoder, 12 self-attention layers, is unchanged and runs once
per object, whatever the number of views.





4.2 The view decoder




The view decoder follows RenderFormer: each image patch is a token built from the directions of the rays through its
pixels, and six layers of cross-attention to the scene tokens and self-attention among the patches turn these tokens
into pixels through a DPT head. Inside it, the Gaussians’ centers are expressed in the camera’s frame and enter the
attention through the same 3D rotary embedding. We make three changes.




2D rotary embedding of projected positions. A 3D position embedding tells the decoder where a Gaussian is relative to the camera, but not where it lands in the
image, which is what a patch needs to know. We therefore project each Gaussian’s center into the view,





ui=1p​(W2+f​xizi),vi=1p​(H2−f​yizi),u_{i}=\frac{1}{p}\Bigl(\frac{W}{2}+f\frac{x_{i}}{z_{i}}\Bigr),\qquad v_{i}=\frac{1}{p}\Bigl(\frac{H}{2}-f\frac{y_{i}}{z_{i}}\Bigr),

(4)


where (xi,yi,zi)(x_{i},y_{i},z_{i}) is the center in camera coordinates, ff the focal length in pixels, W×HW\times H the image
size and pp the patch size, so (ui,vi)(u_{i},v_{i}) is measured in patches. In the cross-attention, the keys of each
Gaussian are rotated by a 2D rotary embedding of (ui,vi)(u_{i},v_{i}) and the query of each patch by the same embedding of
the patch’s center, so the attention between a patch and a Gaussian depends on the distance between them in the
image. The patches’ self-attention uses the same 2D embedding. It rotates 8 channel pairs per image axis in each
attention head, pairs that the 3D embedding leaves unrotated, at 8 frequencies from 0.25 to 1.75 radians per patch,
so the pretrained 3D embedding is kept as it is.




Smaller patches. RenderFormer uses patches of 8×88\times 8 pixels. We use 4×44\times 4 pixels, which gives the decoder four times as
many tokens per image and lets it resolve finer detail. To reuse the pretrained ray embedding, which expects the 64
ray directions of an 8×88\times 8 patch, we upsample each 4×44\times 4 patch’s ray directions to 8×88\times 8 by
nearest-neighbor interpolation before embedding them. This is the same as an embedding of the 48 numbers of a
4×44\times 4 patch whose weights are the pretrained ones summed over each 2×22\times 2 block. The DPT head upsamples
its output to the image size whatever the patch size.




Projected footprint on the keys.  The rotary embeddings tell the decoder where each Gaussian’s center lands, but not how far the Gaussian reaches in
the image. Its scale and rotation reach the decoder only through the scene tokens, which are the same for every view,
so the decoder would have to work out each Gaussian’s apparent size and shape for every camera. We give it directly.
With 𝚺i=𝐑i​diag⁡(𝐬i)2​𝐑i⊤\bm{\Sigma}_{i}=\mathbf{R}_{i}\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{s}_{i})^{2}\mathbf{R}_{i}^{\top} the Gaussian’s
covariance in camera coordinates and 𝐉i\mathbf{J}_{i} the Jacobian of the projection of Eq. 4 at its
center, its footprint in the image, in patches, is the 2×22\times 2 covariance





𝚺i′=𝐉i​𝚺i​𝐉i⊤+0.3p2​𝐈,𝐉i=fp​zi​(10−xi/zi0−1yi/zi),\bm{\Sigma}^{\prime}_{i}=\mathbf{J}_{i}\bm{\Sigma}_{i}\mathbf{J}_{i}^{\top}+\frac{0.3}{p^{2}}\mathbf{I},\qquad\mathbf{J}_{i}=\frac{f}{p\,z_{i}}\begin{pmatrix}1&0&-x_{i}/z_{i}\\
0&-1&y_{i}/z_{i}\end{pmatrix},

(5)


the same projection that 3DGS applies before rasterizing (Kerbl et al., 2023), where the small isotropic term
(0.3 square pixels) is the low-pass filter of the gsplat rasterizer (Ye et al., 2025). Without it the model is only 0.08 dB
worse (Section 6.2). We describe it by four numbers,
the logarithms of its two standard deviations and (cos⁡2​θ,sin⁡2​θ)(\cos 2\theta,\sin 2\theta) for the angle θ\theta of its major
axis, each clipped to [−6,6][-6,6]. In each decoder layer, a linear layer maps these four numbers to a vector that is
added to the Gaussian’s key in the cross-attention, so the footprint changes which Gaussians a patch attends to but
not what it reads from them. Adding it to the values as well helps on our ablation harness, but we keep it in the
keys only for the reasons given in Section 6.2. Register tokens and Gaussians behind the camera get zeros. These linear layers are new
and initialized to zero, so the model starts from exactly the function it had without them.




The projected center and footprint are computed in closed form for each Gaussian from its parameters and the camera,
a change of coordinates like expressing each triangle’s vertices in the camera’s frame for RenderFormer’s decoder. Nothing is
computed per pixel: which Gaussians cover a pixel, which of them are visible, how they blend and what color results
are left to the network.




The first two changes add no parameters and the third only 23,040, initialized to zero, but the first two change what
the pretrained layers compute, which the short stage 3 at 256×256256\times 256 pixels recovers
(Section 4.3).





4.3 Training




The model is trained to reproduce renders of the full, unpruned splat from a version pruned to 20,000 Gaussians
(Section 5). The model outputs colors in the log encoding log10⁡(1+𝐈)\log_{10}(1+\mathbf{I}) inherited from
RenderFormer, and the loss combines an L1 term in that encoding with LPIPS (Zhang et al., 2018) on a VGG backbone:





ℒ=(1−λ)​ℒ1w​(𝐈^,log10⁡(1+𝐈))+λ​ℒLPIPS​(10𝐈^−1,𝐈),λ=0.5,\mathcal{L}=(1-\lambda)\,\mathcal{L}_{1}^{w}\bigl(\hat{\mathbf{I}},\log_{10}(1+\mathbf{I})\bigr)+\lambda\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LPIPS}}\bigl(10^{\hat{\mathbf{I}}}-1,\mathbf{I}\bigr),\qquad\lambda=0.5,

(6)


where 𝐈^\hat{\mathbf{I}} is the model’s output and 𝐈\mathbf{I} the target image. ℒ1w\mathcal{L}_{1}^{w} is the L1
distance averaged over pixels with a weight of 1 on the object and 0.050.05 on the background, since an object covers
only a few percent of the image. Every training sample rotates the object and its camera by the same random rotation, which leaves the image
unchanged but keeps the model from relying on a fixed orientation.




Training runs in four stages (Table 1). The final model’s first two stages differ slightly from this
recipe (Appendix G). The first trains only the new input layer, with the rest of
RenderFormer frozen. The second trains the whole model at
256×256256\times 256 pixels. The third switches on the three changes above for a short recovery at the same resolution,
and the fourth trains at 512×512512\times 512 pixels with LPIPS. We use AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019) with a weight decay of 0.01, a batch of 8
views on 8 GPUs, and a learning rate of 5×10−55\times 10^{-5} (10−310^{-3} in the first stage), held constant after a short
warmup and decayed with a cosine over the last steps of each stage.

5 Data




Source. We train on Objaverse_Splats33
            3
            
            
            
          https://huggingface.co/datasets/ShapeSplats/Objaverse_Splats, the
subset of ShapeSplat with a trained 3DGS splat per Objaverse object. We use only splats that fit their object well according to
ShapeSplat’s metadata, with a PSNR of at least 32 dB and an LPIPS of at most 0.06, and that have exactly 50,000
Gaussians. From these we draw 30,000 objects for training and 2,000 for validation, and drop objects that are
degenerate or nearly transparent (maximum opacity below 0.4). This leaves 26,820 training objects and 1,806
validation objects.




Normalization. Each splat is centered on the median position of its Gaussians, rotated so that its up axis is +y+y, and scaled
uniformly so that its largest coordinate is 0.45, which places every object in [−0.45,0.45]3[-0.45,0.45]^{3}. We keep only the
view-independent color of each Gaussian (Section 3.1).




Ground truth. For every object we render 28 views of the full, unpruned splat with gsplat at 512×512512\times 512
pixels, with a 45∘45^{\circ} field of view and a black background. The cameras orbit the object and look at its center: 14 at
a distance of 1.7, and 7 each at 1.15 and 2.45. Consecutive cameras alternate between looking down at the object and
slightly up at it, to see its top and its bottom. Every object has the same 28 cameras.




Input preparation. The full splats have 50,000 Gaussians, while the model takes 20,000. We keep the 20,000 Gaussians with the highest
importance score adapted from LightGaussian (Fan et al., 2024), which sums each Gaussian’s opacity times its
projected area over 64 orbit views and slightly favors larger Gaussians. Pruning alone leaves holes and darkens the object, so we then fine-tune the kept
Gaussians for 1,500 steps to match renders of the full splat from those 64 views, with the usual 3DGS loss (L1 plus
0.2 times D-SSIM (Wang et al., 2004)) and without adding or removing any Gaussians. This takes about 10 seconds per object on one GPU.
The 64 views lie at the same distance as the main evaluation views, but at different angles. The model never sees
the full splat, only the pruned one, and is trained to reproduce the renders of the full splat. A rasterizer can
render the pruned input directly. We call this gsplat on the input, and use it as the bar for the model throughout
Section 6, while the reference is always the render of the full splat.




Splits. Of the 1,806 validation objects, 100 monitor the validation
loss during training, and 300 others, never seen in training, are the held-out objects on which we evaluated every
design decision. We call them the development held-out set. For the main result, we draw 300 further objects at
random from the remaining 1,406, never used during development: the fresh held-out set. We also evaluate
300 training objects.





6 Experiments




Setup. All images are 512×512512\times 512 pixels with a 45∘45^{\circ} field of view. Both the model’s input and the ground truth use
only the view-independent color of each Gaussian. We report PSNR, SSIM and LPIPS (Zhang et al., 2018), with LPIPS computed on an
AlexNet backbone (the training loss uses VGG). The main result is on the fresh held-out objects, over all 28 views of
the training setup. Unless stated otherwise, every other number is the mean over the 300 development held-out
objects, 4 views each at distance 1.7. Since the object covers only a small part of the image, every metric is
computed on the evaluation crop: the square around the object in the reference (pixels brighter than 0.02), with a
12-pixel margin, resized to 512×512512\times 512. The black background inside the crop is easy for both renderers, so
we also report masked PSNR, over the pixels where the full splat’s opacity exceeds 0.5, on the whole image. We
compare against two references. The first is a rasterization of the full, unpruned splat, which is the image the
model is trained to produce. The second is a rasterization of the pruned 20,000-Gaussian input, which is exactly
what the input encodes.




6.1 Main result




Table 2 shows the main result, on 300 held-out objects that we never used during development. Over
all 28 views at the three training distances, GaussianRenderFormer reaches 38.08 dB against the unpruned render, while
rasterizing the same pruned input reaches 44.02 dB. The gap depends on the camera distance, from 3.9 dB at 1.15 to
6.8 dB at 1.7. Rasterizing the input is best at 1.7, the distance of the 64 views the pruned input was fine-tuned on
(Section 5), while the model is nearly flat across distances. Masked PSNR, which leaves out the black
background, is about 5 dB lower for both, with about the same gap. Figure 3 shows three
held-out objects, at the 10th, 50th and 90th percentile of the model’s score. The model’s errors concentrate on edges
and fine texture, while its colors and shapes are right.




The gap is not one of generalization. On the development held-out objects, on which we chose our design, the model
scores the same, 38.02 dB, so choosing on them did not inflate the result. On 300 training objects, over their 28
training views, it scores only 0.17 dB higher than on unseen objects. These are the exact images the model was
trained to produce, from the exact same input, and it still stays 5.9 dB below rasterizing that input. Nor does the
gap come from what pruning removed: measured against a rasterization of its own input instead of the unpruned
render, the model scores only 0.3 dB higher (4 views at distance 1.7). Its error is in rendering the input it has.
Each training image was seen only about four times in the final stage, though, so an under-trained model would show
the same. Sections 6.3 and 6.4 ask where the gap comes from.

6.2 Ablations




Training the final model took 1,327 GPU-hours, so we do not repeat it for every change. Our earlier probes trained
on 10 objects, and their results did not carry over to larger training sets. We therefore test every change on one
harness of moderate scale. It trains on 1,000 objects from the end of stage 2 (Table 1), with
short versions of stages 3 and 4 (Appendix C.1). Every arm is evaluated on the 300 held-out objects and on its own training objects, and differs from the
harness with all changes, the recipe of the final model, in one change only (Table 4). The end of
stage 2 has 8×88\times 8 patches, no 2D embedding and no footprint, so the arm with all changes has to learn all three
in these steps, while the ablated arms have less to learn. This favors the ablated arms. Every arm is a single run.
To see how far two runs of the same recipe differ, we repeat the arm with all changes with another seed for the data
order, the drawn views and the random rotations. The two runs differ by 0.02 dB on the held-out objects
(Table 4), so differences of a few tenths of a dB between arms are larger than the variation
between runs.

All three changes to how the decoder reads the scene matter. Without the footprint, the model loses 1.7 dB on the
held-out objects, and 299 of the 300 objects are worse. Without the 2D embedding it loses 5.2 dB, and with
8×88\times 8 patches instead of 4×44\times 4 it loses 3.0 dB. Both cost more than before the footprint was added,
4.1 and 2.3 dB on the same harness, so with the footprint the decoder relies more on knowing where each Gaussian
lands and on small patches, not less. Each change costs about as
much on the training objects as on the held-out ones, so they help the model fit its training images, not only
generalize. The changes matter just as much at full scale (Table 7). Trained on all objects with
the recipe before the footprint, to the first milestone of the final run, the model loses 4.3 dB without the 2D
embedding and 2.4 dB with 8×88\times 8 patches. The final recipe without the footprint scores 1.4 dB lower than the
final model on the fresh held-out objects (Table 2), and lower on every one of the 300.
Figure 9 in the appendix shows how each change looks.




Giving the decoder the footprint in its values as well as its keys, through a second zero-initialized layer per
decoder layer, adds another 0.7 dB on the harness (Table 4), so the footprint can also help the decoder read the Gaussians it selects. We nevertheless keep it in the keys only. There,
it only decides which Gaussians a patch attends to, which keeps a clear split between the geometry we compute and
what the network learns. The values variant is also more sensitive to inputs denser than in training
(Sections 6.4 and 6.5): with 40,000 Gaussians, it loses 0.3 dB more than the
keys-only model (95% interval 0.2 to 0.4 dB), and for the keys-only model this loss grows with training
(Section 6.4). We leave the values variant to future work.




Without the small isotropic term
that we take from gsplat (0.3 square pixels), the model loses only 0.08 dB (Table 4), against
1.7 dB without the footprint, so its gain does not come from a rasterizer convention.




Restricting each patch’s cross-attention to the Gaussians that reach its tile, the way a rasterizer bins Gaussians by
tile, adds only 0.2 dB and saves little time (Appendix E), so we keep dense cross-attention
and let the network decide which Gaussians each patch attends to.




The first two stages change the model the most, since they turn a mesh renderer into a Gaussian one, so we test
them separately, at 256 pixels on all 26,820 objects. Each arm trains for 50,000 steps, so all of them get the same
number of steps: from RenderFormer’s weights with the input layer trained alone for the first 5,000 steps (our
recipe), from RenderFormer’s weights with the whole model trained from the start, and from random weights. We evaluate each at 256 pixels on the held-out objects, against the unpruned render
downsampled as in training (Table 10 in Appendix C.4). Training the input layer alone first gives 3.1 dB more
than training the whole model from the start, in the same number of steps. The gain is not the input layer’s higher
learning rate: training the whole model from the start with the input layer at the first stage’s learning rate
(10−310^{-3}) does worse still, 0.55 dB below the whole model at one learning rate. Its validation loss stays flat for
the first 24,000 steps before it starts to learn. We did not find a
recipe that trains the model from random weights: with the loss of stages 1 and 2, it collapsed to a constant black
image.





6.3 Data and training length




To see how much the amount of data matters, we fine-tune the end of stage 2 with the recipe of the harness
(Section 6.2), before the footprint was added, on 1, 10, 100 and 1,000 objects and on all 26,820, with the same number of steps
(Figure 4). Stage 2 has already seen all 26,820 objects, so every point is a fine-tune of a model that
renders thousands of objects, and the passes over each training image differ by four orders of magnitude: about
9,400 at one object and 0.35 at 26,820. On a single object, the model fits its own training images 7 dB above
rasterizing its input and forgets the other objects. With more objects the two curves approach each other and meet at
the full set, about 12 dB below rasterizing the input. At our compute, the model is not data-limited. The final model,
trained about 10 times longer on all objects and with the footprint, still scores about the same on its training and
held-out objects (the triangles).

6.4 Where the gap comes from




Denser inputs than in training do not help the model. With 40,000 Gaussians instead of 20,000,
rasterization gains 4.6 dB while the model loses 3.1 dB (Table 8), and on the real captures,
more Gaussians likewise help the rasterizer and hurt the model (Table 6). It never saw inputs of that size in training. The same recipe
without the footprint also loses 1.6 dB at 40,000 Gaussians, so dense cross-attention, which spreads each patch’s
attention over twice as many Gaussians, is part of the cause, and the footprint, whose values shift as denser splats
use smaller Gaussians, adds to it. An earlier model with windowed cross-attention, in which each patch sees only the
Gaussians that reach it, lost nothing at 40,000 Gaussians, but gained nothing either. With less input, the model
degrades more slowly than rasterization (Table 8), most likely because it was not reproducing
that detail in the first place.




The errors are largest in fine detail. The 10% of pixels with the strongest edges in the reference hold 71% of the
model’s squared error. Yet the model trails the rasterizer by more off the edges than on them, 8.1 against 6.2 dB, so
part of its error lies at low frequencies, at the scale of the object’s parts (Appendix C.8).
At high frequencies it both blurs and adds detail that is not in the reference: above 1/4 cycles per pixel it
reproduces 85% of the reference’s content, against 93% for the rasterizer (Appendix C.9).
The gap also grows with the number of Gaussians the model has to blend at a pixel, and is largest where 32 or more
reach it, as on most of the object (Appendix C.10).




A reachable target does not close the gap, at least late in training. The model is trained to produce renders of the full splat, which hold
detail its pruned input does not. We therefore trained the harness to reproduce the rasterization of its own input
instead, a target that holds nothing the input lacks. Measured against that target, it scores only 0.19 dB more than
the harness trained on the full splat (Appendix C.1). Both are short fine-tunes of a model
that was trained on the full splat, though, so this shows what a reachable target changes late in training, not from the start.




Together, these results are consistent with a bottleneck in the view decoder: the model barely uses more detail when
its input has it, its fine detail is blurred and partly spurious, and a reachable target does not help. The ablations
agree: all three changes that improve the model make the decoder more precise in the image
(Section 6.2), and the footprint gives the most direct evidence. Each Gaussian’s footprint follows
from its center, scale and rotation and the camera, all of which the decoder receives, yet giving it explicitly adds
1.4 dB at full scale, on the held-out and the training objects alike (Table 7). The decoder did not
recover the footprint on its own in our training. It also reduces both kinds of error above at high frequencies: the
model reproduces more of the reference’s content and adds less that the reference does not have
(Appendix C.1).




Under-training is not ruled out, however. The final run was still
improving, each training image was seen about four times, the larger inputs were never seen in training, and the
controls above are short fine-tunes of a model trained on the full-splat target. The evidence that more encoder layers
and a wider network do not help comes from an earlier model on 100 objects (Appendix E).




To test the encoder directly, we fine-tune the final model on the 14 views of one held-out object, once with only
the view decoder trained and the scene encoder frozen, and once with the whole model, for 10,000 steps each. The two
fit the object about equally well, both several dB above rasterizing its input (Appendix C.1).
On one object, the frozen scene tokens hold what the decoder
needs to exceed the rasterizer.




Appendix C.11 shows the four held-out objects where the model does worst. On all four the
shapes are right, and the error comes from dense texture and soft, low-contrast structure.





6.5 Beyond the training setup




One camera configuration. The model was trained in a single camera configuration: 512×512512\times 512 pixels, a 45∘45^{\circ} field of view, and
cameras at distances from 1.15 to 2.45. Table 8 in Appendix C.2
changes one factor at a time, on 30 held-out objects, and shows that the model is a renderer for that configuration, not a general one. At 256 and 1,024 pixels it
drops to about 33 dB. Rasterization also loses about 4 dB at these resolutions, but the model loses about 6. For
cameras much farther away than in training it breaks, at 24.09 dB. Changing the field of view costs only 1.1 dB at
35∘35^{\circ} and 1.2 dB at 60∘60^{\circ}, even when the object fills the frame as in training, while rasterization is
unaffected. Without the footprint, the same model lost 2.4 and 3.2 dB at these fields of view, so telling the decoder how large each Gaussian appears also makes it far less tied to
the training field of view. Cameras closer than in training cost 1.6 dB. Inputs denser than in training do not work
as well: with 40,000 Gaussians the model loses 3.1 dB, against 1.6 dB without the footprint. Varying the resolution, field of view and distance during training would most likely fix the rest. Training on three camera distances instead of one mainly improved close cameras
(Appendix D), and we expect the same for the other factors. Covering more configurations
takes more training.




Camera paths. Rendered along a smooth 96-frame orbit that avoids every training camera, the model keeps its quality, 38.52 dB per
frame on average over 30 held-out objects. It renders every frame on its own and was never trained on sequences, yet
the videos show no flicker (project page, Appendix A).




Real captures. We also apply the model to regions of four real scenes from the Mip-NeRF 360 dataset (Barron et al., 2022), cut
out of splats we train with gsplat and normalized like our objects, with inputs of up to 160,000 Gaussians
(Appendix B). The model gets shapes, colors and layout right and
softens fine texture, as on the objects. With 20,000 Gaussians, its training input size, it stays 1.6 to 4.7 dB below
rasterizing the same input on the evaluation crop. With more Gaussians the rasterizer improves and the model gets
worse (Section 6.4). The regions are viewed from outside on an orbit, so this is not yet rendering of
full scenes.






7 Limitations




Quality and speed. GaussianRenderFormer is not yet a practical replacement for a rasterizer. It stays several dB below rasterizing the same input
(Table 2), is several hundred times slower and needs far more memory (Table 3), and its
time and memory grow quickly with the input (Appendix C.3).




Objects, not scenes. We train and evaluate on single objects viewed from outside, and our real captures are regions of scenes viewed the
same way, so rendering a full scene, with the camera inside it, remains open. The full self-attention of the scene
encoder also limits how many Gaussians the model can take (Appendix C.3).




One camera configuration. GaussianRenderFormer renders well only in its training configuration. Other input sizes, camera distances, resolutions
and fields of view cost it quality, but not the rasterizer (Section 6.5).




Appearance. We use only the view-independent color of each Gaussian, so GaussianRenderFormer cannot render the view-dependent effects that
3DGS stores as spherical harmonics. Unlike RenderFormer, it also renders the colors stored in the Gaussians under
fixed lighting and cannot relight them.




Input preparation. The renderer itself has no rasterizer, but preparing an input from a larger splat does use one. Pruning to 20,000
Gaussians scores each Gaussian by rasterizing it from 64 views, and the following fine-tune is ordinary 3DGS
optimization (Section 5). Inputs of up to 20,000 Gaussians need neither step.




Bound to RenderFormer’s design. Starting from RenderFormer’s weights limits how far the network can change. Changes to its pretrained parts undo what
the weights encode: in early experiments, even doubling the frequency of the existing position embedding hurt as much
as replacing it. Only additive changes, new parts initialized to zero such as our 2D embedding, kept the pretrained
behavior. More capacity on top of the pretrained layers trained without trouble but gave nothing: in an earlier
version of the model, on 100 objects, 9 extra layers (287M parameters in total) and an 8 times wider feed-forward
network performed exactly like the original. We could therefore add to RenderFormer’s design but not freely redesign
it, and we cannot tell how a network designed for Gaussians from the start would do.




Cost and dependence on RenderFormer. Training the model is expensive (Appendix G), and we found no recipe that
trains it from random weights (Section 6.2), so it depends on a pretrained mesh renderer.





8 Conclusion




We presented GaussianRenderFormer, a transformer that renders a given set of 3D Gaussians into an image with no rasterizer in the
renderer. Starting from RenderFormer’s weights, a token per Gaussian, a 2D rotary embedding of where each Gaussian
lands in the image, its projected footprint and smaller patches are enough for a network to render objects it has
never seen, in the camera configuration it was trained in. A clear gap to rasterizing the same input remains. It is not a generalization gap,
and our diagnostics are consistent with a bottleneck in the view decoder, though under-training is not ruled out. A
learned renderer for Gaussians is feasible, but not yet competitive with a rasterizer.




Each limitation in Section 7 points to future work. Giving
each Gaussian material parameters, as relightable Gaussian methods do, and adding light sources to the scene
would let a model like ours learn relighting and global illumination, the goal that motivates this work (Section 1).

Appendix A Camera paths




We render 30 held-out objects along a smooth path of 96 frames that makes one full turn around the object. The
camera distance varies smoothly between 1.35 and 2.05, inside the training range, and the elevation rises and falls
twice per turn. The angles are offset so that no frame repeats a training camera. Every frame is rendered by the
model from the 20,000-Gaussian input, by gsplat from the same input, and by gsplat from the full splat (the
reference).




We report the PSNR of each frame against the reference, on the evaluation crop as in the main evaluation, and
a simple measure of consistency between frames. For each pair of consecutive frames we take the change from one frame
to the next, for the model and for the reference, and compute the PSNR between the two changes over the whole frame.
A model that flickers adds changes the reference does not have and scores lower. This measure does not separate
flicker from ordinary per-frame error, so we compare it with gsplat on the input and also inspect the videos.
Table 5 shows the results and Figure 5 shows frames of one path.

C.8 Error on edges




We rank the pixels of each held-out crop by the Sobel gradient of the gray reference. On the 10% of pixels with the

Appendix D The road to the final model




Several choices in Section 4 only make sense in light of what did not work, so we summarize the
experiments that led to them. Early results used other protocols, so we report most of them only qualitatively.




D.1 Data




Our first training set followed RenderFormer’s examples, meshes sampled into Gaussians with RenderFormer’s own
renders as the target. The model learned to imitate RenderFormer’s lighting instead of rendering its input, so we
switched to real splats rendered by gsplat. Scenes composed of several small splats then plateaued whatever we
changed, and tests on outside captures showed that the quality of the input mattered more than its size. We moved to
single Objaverse_Splats objects, from 2,667 to 13,405 and finally 26,820, and added the fine-tune of the pruned
input (Section 5).





D.2 Training schedule




Training the input layer alone first was part of our design from the start, since RenderFormer’s pretrained layers
expect its own triangle tokens. A series of runs that skipped this stage because of a bug all stalled at the same
loss, whatever encoding of the Gaussian parameters they used, and restoring the stage fixed them. The stage ablation
of Section 6.2 tests this directly. Larger inputs (20,000 Gaussians instead of 5,000), random rotations of every
sample and much longer training with a 256 to 512 pixel curriculum each helped clearly. The best model of this phase,
trained on 13,405 objects, scored 30.39 dB on our held-out set.





D.3 The decoder




That model scored only 0.55 dB better on its training objects than on held-out ones, models trained on 10 to 1,000
objects could not fit even their own training objects, and more layers or a wider network did not help
(Appendix E). We therefore tested changes to the view decoder on 10 training objects. The
2D rotary embedding of projected positions was the first change that improved both training and held-out objects.
Smaller patches improved them further, and became affordable once the view decoder ran in bf16 with gradient
checkpointing, which made a training step about three times faster. We also tried windowed cross-attention at this
point and later dropped it (Section 6.2). Showing
every object from three distances mainly improved close cameras. The final model combines these changes and
trains on all 26,820 objects for 375,000 steps, from the second-stage checkpoint of the previous phase
(Appendix G).

Appendix F Training on a single object




Our main model shares one set of weights across all objects. Here we ask what the same architecture can do when it
only has to render one object, which separates what the network can represent from what it can learn to generalize.
All of these experiments used earlier versions of the model.




Trained on a single object with 9,000 views of its full splat, an earlier version of the model ended above
rasterizing the pruned input on 4 of the 13 objects we tried, by up to 1.8 dB.
It does so by learning the object and filling in what pruning removed, not by rendering its input better: the one
such model we kept scores 44.59 dB against the full splat but only 42.18 dB against a rasterization of its own input.
These runs show how much detail the network can output for one object, not what sharing the weights costs.




F.1 How much detail can the network output




We first trained the model on a single object, a scan of tomatoes, on its 14 training views and without augmentation,
until it had memorized them. On those views it reached 52.5 to 53.0 dB on average against the full splat, with an LPIPS of
0.0003, and its renders cannot be told apart from the reference (Figure 12). The decoder can
therefore output this much detail when it is trained to, so the error of our general model is not a hard limit of
its output head. This experiment used an earlier input: the 219,000-Gaussian scan pruned to 20,000 with the same
importance score, but without the fine-tune of Section 5, so rasterizing it gives a blurry image. On new
views between the training views, the model’s renders are much sharper than that rasterization. They are closer to
the full splat in PSNR (30.5 against 29.7 dB) and far closer in LPIPS (0.09 against 0.15 on the crops of
Figure 12), and far from the rasterization of the model’s own input (27.4 dB). From 14 views of the
full splat, the model learned enough of the object to restore much of what pruning removed. It did not learn all of
it: on new views it stays far below its 52.5 dB on the training views.

F.2 Compensating for pruning on one object




With thousands of views of the object instead of 14, per-object training can beat the rasterizer on new views. The
training images are renders of the full splat, so the model learns that one object and can fill in what pruning
removed from its input. This is a prior on the object, not a better rendering of the input. We trained on 9,000 random views per object, in cycles of 30,000 steps with a cosine learning rate, and
evaluated on 40 views not used in training (24 random, 8 close-up and 8 distant), against gsplat on the same
20,000-Gaussian input. Table 15 and Figure 13 show the results. Only on 4 of the 13
objects we tried did the model end above the rasterizer on average, by up to 1.8 dB, and most of its gain was in
close-up views, where the pruned input shows its gaps. On the other 9 it stayed below, by 1 to 9 dB, typically on
simple objects that the 20,000 Gaussians already render almost perfectly.

F.3 Why we do not pursue it




These results show that the network can output far more detail than our general model does, and that trained on one
object it can learn to fill in what pruning removed. We do not consider per-object training a practical use of it,
for three reasons. It does not transfer to shared weights (Section 6.3). It is
expensive to store and to run: every object needs its own copy of the 195M-parameter network, about 745 MB, to
improve on an input of 20,000 Gaussians that takes 1.1 MB, and rendering a view took 476 ms against 1 ms for gsplat
in our measurement on an NVIDIA A40. It is also slow to train, with 4 to 5 cycles of 30,000 steps per object. We tried
to cut the storage with low-rank adapters (LoRA) on a shared network, but they fell short of the rasterizer at equal
storage. The gain, under 2 dB on the objects where it works, does not justify this cost.






Appendix G Reproducibility




The code, the trained weights, the checkpoint after the first two stages, the lists of training and evaluation
objects, and the scripts for every experiment in this paper are available at
https://github.com/SVLwoof/gaussianformer. Its file paper/README.md gives the command and the expected numbers for every table and
figure. The three trained models are on Hugging Face: the final model (shahafvl/gaussianformer-v22), the
model without the footprint (shahafvl/gaussianformer-v21) and the checkpoint after stage 2
(shahafvl/gaussianformer-256px). Table 1 describes the released
training recipe. The final model was produced by that recipe, with two differences in its first two stages, which
were run during earlier phases of the project. Its first stage was trained on 13,405 objects instead of 26,820, and
its first two stages saw only the 14 views at distance 1.7, not all 28. Starting from the released checkpoint after
the second stage reproduces stages 3 and 4 exactly, which is about 620 of the 1,327 GPU-hours. Running all four stages
from RenderFormer’s weights with the released recipe is documented, but we have not run it end to end.




The stages of the final model took 46 GPU-hours (stage 1) and 662 GPU-hours (stage 2) on NVIDIA L40S GPUs, 592
GPU-hours for stages 3 and 4 up to 375,000 steps (91 on A40 and 501 on RTX Pro 6000 GPUs, as the cluster allowed)
and 27 GPU-hours for the final decay on L40S GPUs.
